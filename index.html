Proper Enclosures

When designing a home for your new ball python, it's important to have their comfort and security at the forefront of your decision making. A snake that feels uncomfortable or unsafe in their environment will be very stressed and could lead to further issues. You will need to decide what type of cage to get, how to keep it properly heated and humid,and what to put in it. This will include the substrate you decide to use, your snake's hides, lights, enrichment items, etc. And of course, a water dish with freshly changed water every few days

The Outside

There are many different containers to choose from for housing your snake and they each have their own pros and cons. The most important things to keep in mind when deciding are its size (big enough that the length of one of the long sides plus one of the short sides is equal to or bigger than the length of your snake), how well it keeps heat and humidity, and its visibility (we want to be able to see them after all). I’ll list three of the most common options for snake cages, with their pros and cons, here.

  • Glass aquarium: A large glass container, usually used as a fish tank, with an open or mesh top. Aquariums are easily accessible, as you can get one almost anywhere, and tend to be cheaper than other options, sometimes even for free from a lucky online ad or garage sale. However, the open top and glass sides are terrible at holding humidity and heat, respectively. Also, the top of the tank lacks any latches or locks to prevent your snake from escaping.
  • Front opening glass cage: Also a large glass container with a mesh top, sometimes sold as a ‘reptile-specific’ tank, but the mesh isn’t removable and it has a front opening door that latches. These are more difficult to find, often requiring going to a reptile shop or ordering online, and are more expensive. The heat and humidity problems are the same as with an aquarium, but the latching door will ensure that your snake stays safely inside.
  • PVC cages: A large, rectangular box made from PVC with a front opening glass or acrylic door(s) that latches. This cage will hold heat, humidity, and your snake itself very well and tends to be the go-to choice for experienced snake owners. However it is the most expensive option and is almost exclusively acquired online which can also add up shipping costs. Although, this seems like a small price to pay to ensure the comfort and safety of your pet who will most likely be with you for 30+ years.

Substrate and Other Interior Items

When picking substrate you want a material that helps hold the tank's humidity and is comfortable for your snake. As long as you're cleaning the enclosure regularly and the material isn't harmful to your snake, you have much more wiggle room in your material selection. I personally prefer coconut husk/coir as you can mix water directly into it for your snake's humidity, but even just paper towels across the bottom will work if properly cleaned and checked regularly.

For heat and light there are many options as well, but for the majority of enclosures, a top down radiant heat panel or ceramic bulb will serve you very well. I like to pair these with a strip of LEDs that can be turned off at night, that way they don't interfere with the heat levels and can support your snake's regulation of their day/night cycle. A regular heat lamp/light can be tricky as you need to turn it off at night for your snake cycle, but then an alternate heating element will be required to keep the enclosure warm. Make sure that no matter what elements you go with, you have a proper thermostat, hygrometer, and temperature gun for measuring the direct space your snake is in to keep temps between 80-90° F and humidity between 60-80%.

Finally we have hides and enrichment items. For hides it’s important to keep in mind that they need to be fully enclosed (besides the hole for the snake to crawl in), they need to be the proper size for your snake so that they fit inside but the walls still hold them quite snuggly, and that you will need at least two identical ones. Your enclosure needs to have one hide on the warm side of the enclosure, under the heating element, and one identical one on the cool side so that your snake can thermoregulate without having to decide between the hide they prefer more and being the proper temperature. As for enrichment, you have unlimited options to add to your snake’s environment. Keep the items safe for your snake and change one or two out every week or so to keep them stimulated, and you're good to go. Even just a new small dollar store fake plant or a paper towel roll will be an adventure for your snake to explore.

How to Feed

Ball pythons can be very picky eaters and can sometimes refuse to eat for months at a time, so it’s important to feed them a proper diet to keep them healthy. The important things to keep in mind when feeding are the size and age of your snake (which determines the size, age, and type of prey item), whether you're feeding live or frozen-thawed, and how often to do so.

Live VS Frozen-Thawed

Picking live or frozen-thawed prey is ultimately up to the keeper, but I personally find frozen-thawed is both safer for your snake and more humane for the prey item. It also tends to be cheaper.

If you’re feeding live, drop the prey item into your snake's enclosure and supervise the interaction in case you need to intervene.

For frozen-thawed, thaw your prey in warm water (in a ziplock if you don’t want to deal with soggy rodents) and then warm the head of the prey with a hair dryer to get it nice and hot before feeding. Use tongs to grip the prey by the scruff and present it to your snake, possibly dancing it around a bit to entice them like a live animal would.

Age to Prey Size Guide

  1. From first shed after hatching: Every 5-7 days, feed them a mouse or rat roughly the size of the thickest part of their body or 10% of their body weight. A large mouse or rat pup will usually do well to start, moving up in rat size as your snake grows.
  2. Once they're large enough to eat weaned rats: move them to eating every 7-10 days, continuing to move up in prey size as needed.
  3. At small rat size: Feed about every 2 weeks. If you go a little over or under on days, just adjust accordingly with their next feeding. For example, if you’re going out of town and need to feed your snake 2 days early, just feed them a little later on their next scheduled day. This is the biggest prey item a male ball python will probably eat for the rest of their life, again about every 2 weeks. They will continue to grow, but after they’re adults, the ‘thickest part of their body/10% of their body weight’ rule no longer applies.
  4. For adult females: they can continue in prey size to medium sized rats as they will grow larger than male ball pythons. Once on medium rats, they can eat them indefinitely about every 2-3 weeks, every 3 weeks once they're a few years old.

For Picky Eaters

Ball pythons can refuse meals for a number of reasons, but there are a few tricks you can utilize to try to convince them to eat. If this is the snake’s first time taking a meal from you, let them settle into their new home for at least five days first, then give them the same type of prey they are used to taking from their previous owner. After a couple of meals, you can then try switching to your preferred prey item and method of feeding if desired. If a snake refuses one of their meals, the first thing to try is to wait a week and then try to feed them again, checking for signs of distress in the meantime. If after a few weeks that doesn’t work and your snake is on rats, try using mice to see if they just have a preference for mice. If this is an adult python and they refuse to eat anything but mice, you will need to adjust to feed them several mice to make sure they're getting the same amount of food as they would with rats. If a frozen-thawed mouse doesn’t do the trick, you can wait a week and attempt a live one instead. Regarding adult ball pythons, they can go for several months at a time without food, so refusing a few meals in a row shouldn't be a cause for worry as long as you've made sure all of their other needs are being met. If all else fails, consult your snake’s veterinarian to see if there might be some other underlying cause for your snake’s loss of appetite.

How to Handle

Intro to handling and why it's important.

General Do's and Don't's

No overhead. Don't plop them back in their enclosure with no warning, let them choose to crawl back in. Don't handle your snake during shed or while they're digesting.

Choice-Based Handling

It can be nice to do 'choice based' handling where the snake decides when to come out therefore building trust.

Training

Target training, tap training, and hook training can also be useful.
